Introduction

It's 12:40 a.m. and the class WhatsApp group is awake. Someone has posted a blurry photo of a hand-written note: "Can anyone explain this balance sheet adjustment before tomorrow's exam?"

Ten minutes later, a friend replies with a screenshot. Not from a textbook — from an AI assistant that walked through the step in Hindi, slowly, the way a patient senior would.

This is what AI in Indian higher education actually looks like. Not a robot lecturer or a shiny lab. A phone, a data pack, and a student who has run out of other options at midnight.

India has about 4.5 crore students in higher education, spread across 48,246 colleges and 1,289 universities, and roughly 70% of responding colleges are private, unaided institutions (AISHE 2023-24 via Business Standard). Most of them are not in IITs or metro campuses. They are in tier-2 and tier-3 towns, in colleges with one computer lab and a library that stops at the 2019 edition.

The interesting question is no longer whether AI has reached Indian colleges. It has. The real question is who gets to use it well — and whether it narrows the gap or quietly widens it.

The Old Divide: Coaching, Libraries and Location

For decades, quality higher education in India followed a map. The best coaching clustered in Kota, Delhi, Hyderabad and a few other hubs. Good libraries, connected faculty and recruiter networks followed the same pin code.

So did confidence. Students at well-known colleges had brand value, internships and alumni who picked up the phone. Students elsewhere had determination and a bus pass.

The job-readiness gap is real, but smaller than the storytelling suggests. The Mercer | Mettl India Graduate Skill Index 2025 put overall graduate employability at 42.6%, down from 44.3% a year earlier. By tier: 48.4% for Tier-I, 46.1% for Tier-II and 43.4% for Tier-III (Business Standard; Mercer).

Roughly five percentage points separate Tier-I from Tier-III. That is a gap worth closing — not a chasm.

What AI changes is the cost of trying to close it.

What Changes When the Tutor Is in Your Pocket

A coaching class runs on a timetable. An AI tutor runs when you do.

That difference sounds small until you are the student who missed a lecture because of a bus strike, or who understood nothing in class and has no one to ask at 10 p.m. AI can explain a concept, generate practice questions, summarise a chapter, check a draft and quiz you — at almost no marginal cost.

Everyday example You miss a Friday lecture on regression. Instead of copying a friend's notes, you paste the topic into an AI tool, ask for a plain-language explanation, then ask for five practice problems. You check the answers against the textbook. The tool got you started; the textbook kept you honest.

Institutions are moving too. The EY-Parthenon–FICCI survey of 30 leading institutions (2025) found that 60% permit student use of AI tools, 53% use generative AI for learning materials, 40% have deployed AI tutors or chatbots, 39% use adaptive learning platforms, and 38% use AI for automated grading (EY; The Hindu).

One caveat matters: those are 30 leading institutions. They show where the top is heading, not what the average tier-3 college looks like today.

Student usage is widespread but harder to pin down. A Digital Education Council survey (2024) found 86% of students use AI in their studies, with 54% using it weekly and 24% daily (EY, citing DEC). That is a global figure, not an India-only one. Needs verification: India-specific student AI usage data at comparable scale.

Language as a Bridge, Not a Barrier

For many students, the hardest part of college is not the subject. It is the language the subject arrives in.

English-medium textbooks, lectures and interviews quietly disadvantage students from vernacular-medium schools — often the same students who are the first in their family to reach college. AI translation and explanation tools are starting to change that.

In May 2025, IGNOU partnered with IIT Bombay's Project Udaan to translate higher-education content into Indian languages (India Today). In November 2025, Masai launched an IIT Patna-backed AI and ML certificate delivered in Hindi, Telugu, Marathi and Tamil, aimed explicitly at tier-2 and tier-3 learners (Telegraph India).

India's own models are part of this. Sarvam 1 supports 11 Indian languages, and BharatGen, led by IIT Bombay, is building public-good AI for Indian languages (Mint).

The quality, though, is uneven. The same Mint report cites a Greyhound CIO Pulse 2025 survey in which 67% of enterprises exploring Indic LLMs reported frequent failures on mixed scripts, regional slang and emotional nuance. A fluent answer can still be a wrong one. Needs verification: independent testing of regional-language AI accuracy in academic subjects.

Everyday example A Tamil-medium student asks for an explanation in Tamil, then asks the same question in English, then checks both against her textbook. She gets the concept twice and builds academic vocabulary along the way.

Placement Prep and the Confidence Gap

Placement season is where the tier gap becomes visible. Well-connected colleges offer mock interviews, resume reviews and alumni referrals. Elsewhere, students apply cold and hope.

AI can partially fill that gap: resume feedback, mock interview questions, aptitude practice, coding explanations, company research. For a student who has never spoken to a recruiter, a low-stakes rehearsal can be the difference between freezing and starting.

There is a signal here. The Mercer | Mettl index found that 46.1% of Indian graduates were employable for AI and ML roles in 2024, up from 40% in 2023, even as overall employability fell (Economic Times). Students are adapting faster than the system around them.

The gender gap deserves naming: female graduate employability was 41.7% against 43.4% for male graduates (Mercer). AI is no cure for structural inequality, but free, private practice helps students who have fewer chances to rehearse in public.

Everyday example You record a two-minute answer to "Tell me about yourself", ask an AI tool to critique the structure, rewrite it, and record again. By the third take, you sound like someone who has done this before — because you have.

Caveat These figures measure assessed employability, not actual hiring. They are not placement rates.

The Other Side: What AI Cannot Fix

A piece about AI levelling the field has to be honest about what it does not touch.

Access is better, but still uneven. The NSS Comprehensive Modular Survey: Telecom (Jan–Mar 2025) found 92.7% of rural and 95.7% of urban persons aged 15–29 used the internet in the previous three months, and 86.3% of households have internet at home (PIB). Real progress.

Capability is the next hurdle. CEDA found 16.7% of rural households lacked internet versus 8.4% urban, only 3.8% of rural households had fibre versus 15.3% urban, and the biggest barrier was digital readiness — about 1 in 2 rural and 2 in 5 urban disconnected households did not know how to use the internet (CEDA, July 2025).

A shared phone is not a laptop. A device shared with three family members, a patchy connection and a capped data pack are not the same as broadband and a quiet room.

Over-reliance is a genuine risk. AI can sound certain and still be wrong — including invented citations. A student who uses it to skip thinking learns less, not more.

Institutions are uneven. The EY-Parthenon–FICCI report itself flags infrastructure, faculty readiness and governance as uneven (EY). A metro college with an AI policy and a tier-3 college with one projector are not starting from the same place.

How Students Can Use AI Well

  1. Ask "why", not just "what". Get the reasoning before the answer.
  2. Verify once, always. Cross-check facts, formulas and citations. If you cannot find the source, treat it as unverified.
  3. Start in your strongest language. Understand it in Tamil or Hindi, then rebuild it in English.
  4. Turn it into a quiz partner. Ask it to test you after each chapter — recall beats re-reading.
  5. Keep a mistake log. Note where AI got things wrong. That is how judgment grows.
  6. Guard your data. Never paste Aadhaar numbers, bank details or private documents into a chatbot.
  7. Know your college's rules. About 57% of surveyed leading institutions already have an AI policy; policies vary, so ask before you submit.

Everyday example Before a group presentation, your team asks an AI tool to play a skeptical examiner and fire questions at you. It is not a perfect stand-in for a professor, but it beats walking in cold.

What Colleges and Policymakers Could Do

  • Teach digital literacy as a core skill, not a one-day workshop. Access without capability does not close gaps.
  • Publish clear AI-use rules so students know what is allowed in assignments and exams.
  • Invest in faculty training, especially where resources are thin.
  • Use the public infrastructure already built. SWAYAM has crossed 4 crore cumulative enrolments (Careers360/UGC); SWAYAM Plus had 4.71 lakh registered learners by December 2025 (Lok Sabha, Feb 2026). The pipes exist; awareness and completion are the hard part.
  • Fund Indian-language academic content. Project Udaan and regional-language AI/ML courses are early steps, not finished solutions.
  • Build in ethics and verification. The IndiaAI Mission, approved in March 2024 with ₹10,371.92 crore over five years, includes a "Safe & Trusted AI" pillar (PIB). Education should benefit explicitly.

Conclusion

The student in that tier-3 town still does not have the library, the labs or the alumni network her metro counterpart has. AI has not handed her a level field.

What it has handed her is a tutor at midnight, an explanation in her own language, and a way to rehearse for interviews she once assumed were not for people like her.

That is not equality. It is a lower entry fee for catching up. And for a motivated student, a lower entry fee is often all that was missing.

The gap will not close because a model got cheaper. It closes when a student opens her phone, asks a better question, checks the answer — and then asks the next one.

FAQs

1. Is AI actually useful for college students in India?

For specific tasks, yes: explaining concepts, generating practice questions, summarising readings, critiquing drafts and rehearsing interviews. It is weakest on precise facts, citations and exam-specific answers you have not verified.

2. Can AI replace coaching classes?

Partly, for some students. It handles doubt-solving and practice cheaply. It cannot replace structured teaching, peer competition, mentorship or accountability. Most students do best with a mix.

3. Are free AI tools enough for exam prep?

Usually, yes. Free tiers cover explanation, practice and feedback. Paid tiers add speed and longer context, but digital access and disciplined use matter more than the subscription.

4. How do I avoid over-relying on AI?

Use an "explain, verify, attempt" loop: ask for the reasoning, check it against a textbook, then solve a similar problem yourself before moving on.

5. Is using AI cheating?

Depends on your institution. About 57% of surveyed leading institutions have an AI policy (EY). Check your college's rules before submitting AI-assisted work.

6. Will AI reduce jobs for Indian graduates?

The evidence is mixed. Overall graduate employability fell to 42.6% in 2024, while AI/ML role readiness rose to 46.1% (Mercer). Expect a shift in which skills matter, not a simple net loss — but this needs watching. Needs verification: longitudinal hiring data by role and tier.
